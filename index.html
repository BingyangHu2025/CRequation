The theorem

Let \(f(z)=u(x,y)+iv(x,y)\), where \(z=x+iy\). Assume \(u\) and \(v\) are differentiable as functions \(\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R\) at \((x_0,y_0)\). Then \(f\) is complex differentiable at \(z_0=x_0+iy_0\) if and only if \[ \boxed{u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x} \] at \((x_0,y_0)\).
Important caveat. The Cauchy–Riemann equations at a single point, by themselves, do not guarantee complex differentiability. The converse uses real differentiability of \(u\) and \(v\) at the point. A common textbook sufficient condition is continuity of the first partial derivatives near the point.

The central idea: complex differentiability means that the first-order real-linear approximation of \(f\) is actually multiplication by one complex number. The Cauchy–Riemann equations are exactly the algebraic conditions that force the real Jacobian to have that form.

Geometric meaning of the CR equations

The real Jacobian is \[ Df= \begin{pmatrix} u_x & u_y\\ v_x & v_y \end{pmatrix}. \] Under the CR equations,

\[ Df= \begin{pmatrix} u_x & -v_x\\ v_x & u_x \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a & -b\\ b & a \end{pmatrix}, \] exactly the real matrix for multiplication by \(a+ib=f'(z_0)\).
Why is this related to conformality?

If \(f'(z_0)\neq0\), multiplication by \(f'(z_0)\) is a rotation followed by a dilation. Thus a holomorphic map with nonzero derivative is infinitesimally angle-preserving.

Why are the CR equations alone at one point not enough?

They constrain only the candidate linear part. They do not guarantee that the remaining nonlinear error is \(o(|\Delta z|)\). Real differentiability supplies exactly that control.

Common textbook sufficient hypothesis

If the first partial derivatives exist in a neighborhood, are continuous at the point, and satisfy the CR equations there, then \(u\) and \(v\) are differentiable there, so the sufficiency argument applies.
